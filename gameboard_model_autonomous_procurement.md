## 1. Core Positioning

### Autonomous Procurement Gameboard Model

Help procurement professionals, buyers, sourcing specialists, category managers, supplier managers, contract specialists, procurement operations leaders, analysts, SAP Ariba practitioners, consultants, product owners, architects, and students progress from understanding procurement concepts to designing, validating, and improving responsible procurement outcomes.

**Core reframe:** A course explains procurement. A gameboard helps learners solve procurement problems, produce reviewable artifacts, demonstrate capability, and improve the operating outcome.

The learner is the **player**; the procurement transformation result is the **winning promise**; capability milestones are **levels**; applied work is organised as **missions**; verified progress is recognised through the **reward engine**; and reviewed outcomes feed the continuous **growth loop**.

The gameboard is not just points, badges, or leaderboards. It connects learning to business need, sourcing decisions, supplier relationships, contracts, purchasing processes, risk and controls, spend insight, system dependencies, and measurable outcomes.

### Positioning line

**“Turn procurement learning into missions that create measurable business and supplier value.”**

Supporting messages:
- “Do not just process a purchase. Design the procurement outcome.”
- “From business need to sourcing decision, supplier delivery, and value.”
- “Learn the process. Design the control. Validate the evidence. Own the outcome.”
- “Build procurement capability through decisions and artifacts—not content consumption alone.”
- “Connect spend, suppliers, contracts, operations, risk, and responsible automation.”

## 2. Six Core Concepts Adapted for Procurement

| Gameboard concept | Autonomous Procurement interpretation | Learner output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Player Identity Blueprint | Define present procurement skills, target role, category or process focus, experience, and capability gaps | Capability baseline and target-role map |
| 2. Winning Promise Formula | Define the business need, procurement problem, intended value, constraints, risk appetite, and acceptance criteria | Outcome statement and value/measurement plan |
| 3. Level Ladder System | Progress from procurement awareness through applied process work, solution design, validated execution, and responsible innovation | Progression map and mastery evidence |
| 4. Mission Design Blueprint | Turn procurement concepts into realistic sourcing, supplier, contract, requisition, purchase-order, invoice, risk, and analytics scenarios | Sourcing strategy, supplier scorecard, process map, control matrix, contract risk register, test pack, dashboard, or prototype |
| 5. Reward Engine Formula | Recognise sound commercial judgement, evidence-based supplier evaluation, control quality, collaboration, traceability, and verified improvement | Rubric result, reviewed portfolio, achievement, or showcase |
| 6. Growth Loop System | Use reviewed artifacts and lessons to improve missions, procurement playbooks, peer learning, controls, category strategies, and future cases | Reusable template, revised playbook, case study, or improvement proposal |

### 2.1 Player Identity Blueprint

Ask the learner:
- Which procurement activities can you explain and perform confidently today?
- Can you translate a stakeholder need into a clear demand or sourcing requirement?
- Can you distinguish strategic sourcing, supplier selection, purchasing execution, contract management, supplier performance, and procure-to-pay controls?
- Can you evaluate offers using declared criteria rather than price alone?
- Can you identify conflicts of interest, supplier concentration, continuity, compliance, sustainability, and data-quality risks?
- Can you connect procurement process decisions to ERP, sourcing, supplier, contract, finance, risk, and analytics systems?
- Which role are you developing toward: buyer, sourcing analyst, category manager, supplier relationship manager, contract specialist, procurement operations lead, SAP Ariba consultant, procurement product owner, integration specialist, procurement architect, data/analytics practitioner, or AI-enabled transformation leader?
- Which category, industry, procurement process, and supplier context will ground the learning?

**Deliverable:** A capability baseline, target role, selected procurement domain, and one problem to solve.

### 2.2 Winning Promise Formula

Avoid vague promises such as “learn procurement” or “understand SAP Ariba.” State what the learner will produce or demonstrate.

Examples:
- Create a category sourcing strategy with business requirements, market assumptions, evaluation criteria, risks, commercial options, and a recommendation.
- Build a supplier evaluation scorecard balancing total cost, quality, delivery, risk, sustainability, service, and the approved requirements.
- Map a requisition-to-purchase-order process and identify approval, data, policy, and exception gaps.
- Create a supplier onboarding and due-diligence checklist with evidence, ownership, risk tiers, and escalation triggers.
- Analyse a fictional spend cube to identify concentration, maverick buying, fragmented demand, and savings hypotheses—with caveats on data quality.
- Design a contract lifecycle control map covering obligations, renewals, expiry, amendments, and accountable owners.
- Build a procure-to-pay integration test pack covering purchase-order changes, goods receipt, invoice matching, duplicate messages, errors, reconciliation, and recovery.
- Design a responsible AI-assisted procurement workflow with bounded use cases, permitted data, human decision rights, evaluation scenarios, audit trail, and fallback handling.

A well-formed promise identifies **learner/persona + procurement problem + artifact or capability + acceptance criteria + measurement method**.

### 2.3 Level Ladder System

Use the SuccessLabs Academy's evidence-based progression pattern while aligning the exact labels to the membership or learning program in which this procurement journey will be delivered:

- **Bronze — Discover / Understand:** Learn procurement vocabulary, lifecycle stages, stakeholder roles, policy and control basics; complete a guided observation or process sketch.
- **Silver — Learn / Apply:** Work through a structured sourcing, supplier, contract, or purchasing scenario; create a first artifact and explain the reasoning.
- **Gold — Apply / Architect:** Design an end-to-end solution, compare options, model risks, validate process or integration behavior, and defend trade-offs.
- **Diamond — Build / Lead:** Demonstrate cross-functional ownership, governance, supplier/business engagement, operational readiness, and value realisation through a reviewed capstone.
- **Quantum — Experiment & Invent, where supported:** Prototype and evaluate emerging procurement analytics, automation, or AI patterns with clear controls, test evidence, and human accountability.

Use the official tier definitions configured in the relevant SuccessLabs Academy program. This document maps capabilities illustratively; it does not create a new certification or override existing membership rules.

**Journey summary:** Understand the business need. Design the sourcing or purchasing response. Validate controls and delivery. Own value and supplier outcomes. Improve responsibly.

### 2.4 Mission Design Blueprint

Each procurement mission should contain:
1. **Business context:** organisation, spend category, business need, supplier market, timing, constraints, and industry.
2. **Evidence pack:** fictional requests, quotes, category spend, supplier performance, contract clauses, purchase orders, receipt records, invoice exceptions, policy extracts, or risk information.
3. **Challenge:** a sourcing decision, supplier issue, process defect, control weakness, commercial trade-off, or value opportunity.
4. **Action:** analyse, compare, map, negotiate in simulation, design, test, report, or prototype.
5. **Artifact:** the tangible learner output.
6. **Quality bar:** published rubric, acceptance criteria, risk/control requirements, and evidence traceability.
7. **Reflection:** assumptions, exceptions, commercial trade-offs, uncertainty, ethical implications, and next improvement.

Use fictional or properly approved anonymised data. Do not include real supplier bank details, confidential bids, restricted contract terms, credentials, personal data, protected negotiation strategies, or confidential client spend in public learning assets.

### 2.5 Reward Engine Formula

Recognise demonstrated procurement capability rather than activity volume alone:
- **Need Clarifier:** converts a stakeholder request into a defined, testable business requirement.
- **Sourcing Strategist:** compares options using criteria, evidence, market assumptions, and trade-offs.
- **Supplier Steward:** designs proportionate onboarding, performance review, and relationship practices.
- **Commercial Value Analyst:** explains total-cost drivers and separates verified value from savings hypotheses.
- **Contract Guardian:** identifies obligations, milestones, renewal risks, and accountable owners.
- **Control Architect:** connects procurement risks to approvals, segregation of duties, evidence, and exception paths.
- **P2P Flow Validator:** tests purchase-order, receipt, invoice, and payment-related process paths, including failures.
- **Spend Insight Translator:** produces a traceable spend analysis with definitions, data-quality limits, and decision relevance.
- **Responsible Procurement AI Builder:** demonstrates defined scope, permission boundaries, evaluation, human review, and auditability.
- **Knowledge Contributor:** publishes a reviewed reusable asset or improves a procurement mission.

Recognition must follow transparent criteria. A badge should not be described as a professional certification unless a formal, governed assessment supports that claim.

### 2.6 Growth Loop System

**Mission completed → artifact reviewed → commercial/process/control reasoning checked → feedback captured → artifact improved → approved reusable asset published → next mission refined.**

Strong artifacts can become worked examples, sourcing templates, process maps, supplier scorecards, control patterns, test cases, or research prompts after review. Keep confidential supplier information, individual data, live bids, bank details, pricing agreements, and proprietary negotiation content out of shared learning materials.

## 3. The Eight Learning Formats for Autonomous Procurement

Choose the format according to learner intent, depth, interaction, and expected output. Durations are indicative planning ranges, not fixed standards.

| Format | Student question | Primary purpose and depth | Indicative duration | Interaction | Procurement example and student output | Best for / what it is not |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Webinar** | “What is this procurement topic and why does it matter?” | Awareness, orientation, and quick understanding; introductory to intermediate | 30–60 minutes, usually one session | Low–moderate: Q&A, polls, chat | **From Business Need to Better Buying:** introduce the procurement lifecycle, stakeholder alignment, sourcing choices, supplier risk, and value. Output: a need statement and next-step checklist. | Beginners or mixed audiences. Not hands-on sourcing mastery or a build sprint. |
| **2. MasterClass** | “How does an expert make procurement decisions in complex situations?” | Expert-led patterns, category strategy, supplier risk, commercial trade-offs, and governance | 2–6 hours or a short expert series | Moderate–high: case walkthrough, critique, examples, Q&A | **Strategic Sourcing MasterClass:** evaluate a fictional category with competing cost, quality, continuity, sustainability, and supplier-risk considerations. Output: sourcing decision framework and evaluation checklist. | Learners with prior knowledge. Not a multi-week implementation journey. |
| **3. Workshop** | “How do I practise this one procurement skill?” | Focused practice on one process, tool, decision, or artifact | 1–3 hours, usually one session | High: guided exercises, practice, facilitator feedback | **Supplier Scorecard Studio:** score three fictional supplier proposals using pre-agreed weighted criteria and document evidence, assumptions, conflicts, and recommendation. Output: completed scorecard and decision rationale. | Learners who need one applied skill. Not broad awareness or multi-week mastery. |
| **4. Bootcamp** | “How do I build complete procurement capability step by step?” | Structured development across multiple concepts and milestones | Multi-day or multi-week journey; around 2 hours/day is one planning option | High: practice, coaching, reviews, accountability | **Source-to-Contract Architect Bootcamp:** move from business requirements and category strategy to event design, evaluation, negotiation simulation, contract handover, supplier onboarding, and performance governance. Output: connected sourcing portfolio. | Committed learners building systematic capability. Not a single session or inspiration-only event. |
| **5. Hackathon** | “Can I solve a procurement challenge and prove it with evidence?” | Challenge-based creation, problem-solving, innovation, and demonstration | Intensive sprint or recurring mission cycle; can be designed as six missions over 90 days | Very high: teams, mentors, peer review, demos | **Procurement Value Challenge:** solve a fictional spend, supplier continuity, onboarding, contract visibility, or P2P exception problem. Output: prototype, control design, spend analysis, test pack, case study, or working artifact. | Learners ready to demonstrate execution. Not teaching-first or passive discussion. |
| **6. Challenge (21 days, gamified)** | “How do I build consistent procurement practice and show progress?” | Habit-building through short daily missions, checkpoints, and feedback | 21 days with daily tasks and progress tracking | Very high: daily prompts, submissions, peer accountability, recognition | **21 Days of Procurement Judgement:** clarify a requirement, identify a supplier risk, inspect a fictional quote, spot a contract obligation, classify a P2P exception, or question a savings assumption. Output: progress log, micro-artifacts, and final reflection. | Repetition and confidence-building. Not a one-off event or deep implementation bootcamp. |
| **7. Sprint** | “What procurement deliverable can I finish in a short execution window?” | Time-boxed execution of one narrowly defined outcome | 1–7 days, usually one outcome | High: check-ins, blocker removal, rapid feedback | **Five-Day Supplier Risk Sprint:** create a risk-tiered supplier review matrix with indicators, evidence, owner, escalation, and review cadence. Output: one reviewed matrix and response plan. | Learners who know the basics and need momentum. Not broad teaching or open-ended exploration. |
| **8. SuperClass** | “What is the highest-level way to think about autonomous procurement?” | Premium strategic framing, enterprise judgement, governance, and leadership principles | 60–180 minutes or a short premium series | Moderate: expert lecture, cases, Q&A, reflection | **Autonomous Procurement Strategy SuperClass:** explore connected spend intelligence, supplier ecosystems, contract-to-operation linkage, responsible sourcing, orchestration, AI, controls, and value ownership. Output: enterprise principles and executive decision questions. | Experienced learners seeking strategic clarity. Not a practice lab, accountability program, or build competition. |

### Format selection rule

- Need awareness and shared vocabulary? **Webinar**.
- Need expert judgement and complex commercial trade-offs? **MasterClass** or **SuperClass**.
- Need one focused skill practised? **Workshop**.
- Need connected sourcing or procurement capability built? **Bootcamp**.
- Need a demonstrable solution to a challenge? **Hackathon**.
- Need regular practice and reflection? **21-day Challenge**.
- Need one defined deliverable completed quickly? **Sprint**.

A format is not a curriculum by itself. Combine formats intentionally and make each deliverable and assessment proportionate to the format.

## 4. Procurement Gameboard Examples by Learning Format

### 4.1 Webinar — From Business Need to Better Buying

**Audience:** New buyers, business stakeholders, students, and cross-functional teams.  
**Question:** What should happen between a business need and a responsible purchase?

**45-minute outline**
- 0–5 min: introduce a fictional department request with unclear requirements.
- 5–15 min: explain need clarification, sourcing route, supplier selection, contract, purchase order, receipt, and invoice flow.
- 15–25 min: poll learners about cost, risk, quality, urgency, and policy implications.
- 25–35 min: demonstrate a procurement decision map.
- 35–42 min: Q&A and reflection.
- 42–45 min: offer a supplier-scorecard workshop or sourcing mission.

**Deliverable:** A clarified need statement and one procurement risk/question.  
**Success measure:** Learners can distinguish the business need, sourcing decision, approval path, and evidence still required.

### 4.2 MasterClass — Strategic Sourcing Decision Lab

**Audience:** Sourcing specialists, category managers, procurement leads, and experienced consultants.  
**Scenario:** A fictional organisation must source a critical service. One supplier offers the lowest price, another offers stronger continuity and service, and a third has better sustainability evidence but limited capacity.

**Activities:** Review requirements, compare evaluation models, challenge assumptions about total cost, analyse supplier dependencies, consider conflicts of interest, and debate commercial/operational trade-offs.

**Deliverable:** Sourcing recommendation, risk-adjusted comparison, and decision checklist.  
**Success measure:** The learner explains how the recommendation follows the published criteria and evidence instead of relying on headline price alone.

### 4.3 Workshop — Supplier Scorecard Studio

**Audience:** Learners practising supplier evaluation.  
**Scenario:** Three fictional proposals respond to a defined business requirement with different price, quality, delivery, support, resilience, and sustainability profiles.

**Steps:** Confirm mandatory requirements → define weighted criteria → score supplied evidence → record unknowns → run sensitivity checks → document the recommendation and approvals required.

**Deliverable:** Supplier scorecard, evidence log, and decision rationale.  
**Review criteria:** Criteria are relevant and declared up front; scoring is traceable; mandatory conditions are distinguished from weighted criteria; conflicts and missing evidence are surfaced.

### 4.4 Bootcamp — Source-to-Contract Architect

**Audience:** Learners building a connected procurement capability across strategy, sourcing, contracts, and supplier governance.

**Suggested 10-session / two-week sequence**
1. Business demand, stakeholder outcomes, and requirement quality.
2. Category context, spend baseline, and market assumptions.
3. Sourcing strategy, route-to-market, and governance.
4. Event design, supplier invitation, and evaluation criteria.
5. Offer analysis, clarification, and negotiation simulation.
6. Supplier risk, due diligence, and responsible sourcing.
7. Contract structure, obligations, milestones, and renewal ownership.
8. Purchase requisition/order handoff and source-to-pay dependencies.
9. Supplier performance metrics, issue resolution, and continuous improvement.
10. Capstone presentation and procurement architecture review.

**Deliverable:** Requirement brief, sourcing strategy, scorecard, risk register, contract obligation map, handover checklist, and supplier-performance plan.  
**Success measure:** Artifacts connect the business need to the supplier decision, contractual obligations, execution process, and measures.

This is a proposed generic sequence. A specialist journey such as indirect services, direct materials, IT sourcing, facilities, or public procurement should adapt its scenarios, governance, and criteria.

### 4.5 Hackathon — Procurement Value Challenge

**Audience:** Cross-functional teams prepared to solve a bounded procurement problem.  
**Challenge options:** Supplier risk visibility, contract renewal blind spots, duplicate or fragmented spend, purchasing-policy exceptions, supplier onboarding delay, or P2P match exceptions.

**Six-mission pattern**
- Mission 1: define the business problem, affected stakeholders, and potential exposure.
- Mission 2: inspect a synthetic spend/supplier/process evidence pack and identify data limitations.
- Mission 3: map the current process, dependencies, risks, and decision rights.
- Mission 4: design a workflow, scorecard, dashboard, process change, or prototype.
- Mission 5: test normal, exception, and failure paths against published criteria.
- Mission 6: demonstrate the artifact, value hypothesis, controls, limitations, and next steps.

**Deliverable:** Solution artifact, evidence log, demonstration, and value-realisation hypothesis.  
**Assessment:** Business relevance, commercial reasoning, supplier fairness, data quality, risk and control coverage, usability, feasibility, and responsible automation.

Clearly label simulated savings or prototype outcomes. Do not claim realised savings, compliance, or supplier-risk reduction without a credible baseline and verified evidence.

### 4.6 21-Day Challenge — Daily Procurement Judgement

**Audience:** Learners building practical judgement through short repeated exercises.

**Weekly themes**
- Days 1–7: clarify needs, stakeholders, requirements, and sourcing choices.
- Days 8–14: evaluate supplier evidence, risk, contracts, and controls.
- Days 15–21: interpret spend and performance, handle exceptions, and communicate recommendations.

**Sample missions**
- Rewrite a vague business request into a testable requirement.
- Identify a hidden assumption in a supplier quote.
- Compare total-cost factors in a fictional bid.
- Spot a supplier due-diligence gap using a supplied evidence pack.
- Identify a renewal or obligation risk in a fictional contract summary.
- Classify a purchase-order/invoice mismatch and identify the next evidence needed.
- Write a recommendation that separates facts, assumptions, risks, and unknowns.

**Deliverable:** 21-day progress log, micro-artifacts, and a final procurement case reflection.  
**Success measure:** Improvement in the quality and consistency of learner reasoning—not streak length alone.

### 4.7 Sprint — Five-Day Supplier Risk Sprint

**Audience:** Learners who know the basics and need to finish one deliverable.  
**Outcome:** Produce a reviewed, risk-tiered supplier review matrix for one category or supplier scenario.

- Day 1: define the scope, risk dimensions, and review objective.
- Day 2: define indicators, evidence sources, and data-quality caveats.
- Day 3: assign owners, review cadence, thresholds, and escalation routes.
- Day 4: test the matrix against fictional supplier scenarios and edge cases.
- Day 5: peer-review and revise the matrix and response plan.

**Deliverable:** Supplier risk matrix with review and escalation guidance.  
**Success measure:** Material risks have explicit indicators, evidence, an accountable owner, and an appropriate response.

### 4.8 SuperClass — Autonomous Procurement Strategy

**Audience:** Procurement leaders, enterprise architects, sourcing/category leaders, transformation sponsors, and experienced practitioners.

**Strategic question:** How does procurement evolve from transactional purchasing to an intelligent, connected, responsible, and value-oriented enterprise capability?

Topics:
- Procurement as a business capability ecosystem, not only a purchasing toolset.
- Need → Sourcing → Supplier → Contract → Purchase → Receive → Invoice → Insight → Decision → Improvement.
- Spend data, supplier identity, category intelligence, and data-quality foundations.
- Source-to-contract and procure-to-pay continuity.
- Supplier resilience, concentration, sustainability, ethics, and responsible sourcing.
- AI-assisted analysis and workflow orchestration with human decision rights, auditability, and exception handling.
- Benefits management: distinguishing negotiated savings, cost avoidance, realised value, and hypotheses.

**Deliverable:** Procurement architecture principles, executive decision checklist, and a prioritised transformation-question set.  
**Success measure:** Learners identify the capability, process, data, integration, governance, and operating-model changes needed before automating a procurement activity.

## 5. Procurement Gameboard — Worked Example: Own the Source-to-Contract Outcome

**Player:** Sourcing analyst, buyer, category manager, SAP Ariba consultant, procurement product owner, or aspiring procurement architect.  
**Business challenge:** A fictional organisation has inconsistent requirements, a rushed supplier selection, incomplete due diligence, unclear contract handover, and limited visibility of supplier performance.  
**Winning promise:** Produce a traceable source-to-contract blueprint that connects business need, sourcing decision, supplier risk, contract obligations, operational handover, and value measures.

| Mission | Learner action | Artifact / evidence | Example quality check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Discover the need | Review a fictional request and stakeholder context | Need statement and stakeholder map | Need, scope, constraints, and success criteria are explicit |
| 2. Define sourcing strategy | Analyse category context and sourcing options | Sourcing strategy and assumptions | Route-to-market rationale fits the need and constraints |
| 3. Design evaluation | Establish requirements, mandatory criteria, and weighted criteria | Evaluation matrix | Criteria are disclosed, relevant, and consistently applied |
| 4. Evaluate suppliers | Review fictional quotes, due-diligence evidence, and risk information | Scorecard and evidence log | Scores trace to evidence; missing data is not treated as proof |
| 5. Decide and negotiate | Compare options and document the decision and negotiation objectives | Recommendation and decision log | Trade-offs, approvals, conflicts, and unknowns are visible |
| 6. Manage contract handover | Map obligations, dates, deliverables, owners, and renewal triggers | Obligation map and handover checklist | Obligations have owners and follow-up mechanisms |
| 7. Connect execution | Map requisition, purchase order, receipt, invoice, and relevant system handoffs | Process/integration map | Exceptions, data ownership, and recovery paths are addressed |
| 8. Measure supplier outcomes | Define cost, quality, delivery, risk, service, and value measures | Supplier scorecard and review cadence | Each measure has a definition, source, owner, and limitation |
| 9. Present the case | Explain recommendation, value hypothesis, risks, and implementation needs | Stakeholder presentation | Recommendation is evidence-based and balanced |
| 10. Improve and share | Incorporate reviewer feedback and record reusable lessons | Versioned portfolio artifact | Revisions link to review comments, test evidence, or new facts |

### Possible measures

- Requirement completeness before sourcing begins.
- Evaluation evidence completeness and scoring consistency.
- Supplier onboarding cycle time and unresolved due-diligence items.
- Contract obligations with explicit owner and due date.
- Purchase-order compliance and avoidable exception rates.
- Supplier quality, delivery, service, or continuity indicators.
- Spend classification coverage and data-quality exceptions.
- Benefits tracked against a defined baseline and agreed calculation method.

Do not assume every procurement case has a savings outcome. Value may include service continuity, risk reduction, quality, innovation, sustainability, avoided disruption, or improved compliance. Define how each intended benefit will be measured.

## 9. Webinar / MasterClass Strategy

### Recommended title

**Autonomous Procurement Gameboard MasterClass: From Business Need to Value Ownership**

### Promise

“Learn how to turn a procurement problem into connected missions that produce a defensible sourcing decision, supplier/control evidence, and a measurable value plan.”

### Three teaching points

**1. The Identity Shift — From Buyer to Procurement Problem Owner**
- Identify the target role and capability.
- Connect stakeholder requirements to sourcing and delivery outcomes.
- Separate evidence, assumptions, risks, and unknowns.

**2. The Mission Ladder — From Need to Supplier Outcome**
- Break a procurement problem into missions.
- Produce an artifact at each milestone.
- Validate criteria, controls, handover, and exception paths.

**3. The Evidence Loop — From Sourcing Decision to Continuous Improvement**
- Trace selection and process decisions to evidence.
- Use peer or mentor review.
- Improve the case and publish approved reusable assets.

**Attendee takeaway:** A one-page Procurement Gameboard Canvas for a chosen procurement problem.

- **Supplier Steward:** demonstrates proportionate due diligence and performance thinking.
- **Contract Guardian:** makes obligations, dates, risk, and owners explicit.
- **P2P Flow Validator:** tests normal and failure paths with traceable evidence.
- **Spend Insight Translator:** presents transparent analysis and qualified value hypotheses.
- **Responsible Procurement AI Builder:** demonstrates defined permissions, evaluations, human review, and auditability.
- **Procurement Innovation Showcase:** presents a reviewed artifact with limits stated.

Keep completion, proficiency, certification, and business impact distinct. Participation does not automatically confer mastery or professional certification.

## 11. Community Code of Honor for Autonomous Procurement

1. **Integrity** — Represent supplier evidence, pricing, savings, and evaluation results honestly.
2. **Fairness** — Apply declared criteria consistently and manage conflicts of interest.
3. **Ownership** — Make decision rights, actions, contract obligations, and follow-up explicit.
4. **Curiosity** — Clarify demand and investigate root causes before choosing a supplier or automating a process.
5. **Stewardship** — Protect supplier confidentiality, personal data, commercial information, and access boundaries.
6. **Collaboration** — Respect stakeholders, suppliers, peers, and reviewers.
7. **Responsible Innovation** — Keep human accountability for material sourcing and supplier decisions; validate AI outputs.

### Accountability partner loop

1. **Commit:** choose one procurement artifact, the acceptance criteria, and a deadline.
2. **Check in:** share evidence, blockers, assumptions, and open risks.
3. **Reflect and reset:** assess quality, document lessons, and select the next mission.

A missed target honestly analysed can become a useful case. Concealing a conflict, fabricating supplier evidence, or claiming unsupported savings must never be treated as winning.

## 12. The 30-Day Autonomous Procurement Pilot

### Week 1 — Define the gameboard
- Choose one bounded challenge, such as supplier evaluation or onboarding risk.
- Define learner persona, baseline, business problem, winning promise, and rubric.
- Prepare a fictional request, supplier proposals, and evidence pack.

**Deliverable:** Procurement Gameboard Canvas and case brief.

### Week 2 — Build missions
- Create 4–6 missions, each with a tangible artifact.
- Define mandatory requirements, score rubrics, exception cases, and quality checks.
- Include supplier fairness, confidentiality, risk, and responsible AI guardrails where relevant.

**Deliverable:** Pilot mission pack.

### Week 3 — Run the pilot
- Invite a small cohort of learners or a cross-functional group.
- Observe completion, artifact quality, time-to-decision, clarification requests, and reviewer feedback.
- Ask about relevance, workload, confidence, and whether instructions made decision criteria clear.

**Deliverable:** Pilot evidence log and a prioritised issue list.

### Week 4 — Review and improve
- Score artifacts with the published rubric.
- Compare first drafts with final submissions.
- Identify unclear requirements, missing evidence, scoring ambiguity, and avoidable rework.
- Version the missions and record the reason for each change.

**Deliverable:** Version 2 of the procurement gameboard and review summary.

## 13. Measures That Matter

| Dimension | Example measure | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Engagement | Mission start and completion rates | Whether learners begin and finish the work |
| Learning | Baseline-to-final rubric change | Whether demonstrated capability improved |
| Requirement quality | Requirements that are complete, testable, and approved before sourcing | Whether procurement begins with a clear business need |
| Supplier decision quality | Scores traceable to agreed criteria and evidence | Whether evaluation is consistent and defensible |
| Risk and control quality | Material risks mapped to evidence, owners, controls, and response actions | Whether the design recognises important exposures |
| Contract ownership | Obligations with named owners, dates, and monitoring approach | Whether contract handover is operationally usable |
| P2P process quality | Purchase-order compliance, matching exceptions, or repeated process defects | Whether execution is working as intended |
| Supplier performance | Relevant quality, delivery, service, continuity, or corrective-action measures | Whether supplier outcomes are being monitored |
| Spend insight quality | Classification coverage, duplicate/fragmented spend signals, and transparent assumptions | Whether analysis is reliable enough to inform a decision |
| Value realisation | Baseline, target, observed result, calculation rules, scope, and limitations | Whether value claims are credible |
| Responsible automation | Evaluation coverage, human-review boundaries, logs, and fallback behavior | Whether automation is designed and tested responsibly |

Do not optimise for the lowest unit price, fastest sourcing cycle, or projected savings alone. A good procurement decision considers the stated need, total cost, service/quality, supply continuity, compliance, sustainability where relevant, and evidence quality.

Distinguish negotiated savings, cost avoidance, budget variance, price changes, demand changes, and realised value. They are not interchangeable; define the calculation before reporting a benefit.

## 14. Responsible Procurement Learning Rules

- **Protect commercial confidentiality.** Use fictional or approved anonymised bids, supplier records, contracts, and spend data.
- **Avoid conflicts of interest.** Disclose and manage relationships that could distort sourcing decisions.
- **Apply criteria fairly.** Define evaluation criteria before scoring; document mandatory gates and weightings where appropriate.
- **Do not equate cheapest with best.** Make total cost, quality, delivery, resilience, service, compliance, and other relevant criteria explicit.
- **Respect policy and jurisdiction.** Procurement obligations vary by organisation, industry, contract, and applicable rules; state assumptions rather than inventing requirements.
- **Separate simulation from real outcomes.** Label hypothetical savings, synthetic supplier performance, and prototype results accurately.
- **Keep controls intact.** Do not teach learners to bypass approvals, delegation of authority, segregation of duties, supplier due diligence, or records retention.
- **Use AI with safeguards.** Define permitted data, evaluation criteria, human review for material decisions, audit logging, monitoring, and escalation.
- **Make criteria transparent.** Learners should know what counts as completion, proficiency, and quality.

## 15. Reusable Autonomous Procurement Gameboard Canvas

Complete this before designing a course, learning journey, sprint, challenge, or hackathon.

1. **Player:** Who is learning and which target procurement role/capability is relevant?
2. **Domain:** Is the challenge about requirements, category strategy, sourcing, suppliers, contracts, P2P, spend, risk, integration, or AI?
3. **Starting point:** What can the learner already do and where is the gap?
4. **Business context:** What need, category, supplier market, timeline, industry, and constraints are in scope?
5. **Problem:** Which commercial, process, supplier, control, data, or decision issue matters?
6. **Winning promise:** What artifact or capability will demonstrate progress?
7. **Evidence and criteria:** Which source pack, mandatory requirements, scoring rules, controls, and rubric will be used?
8. **Learning format:** Webinar, MasterClass, Workshop, Bootcamp, Hackathon, 21-day Challenge, Sprint, or SuperClass—and why?
9. **Missions:** What will the learner clarify, analyse, compare, negotiate in simulation, design, test, present, or improve?
10. **Dependencies:** Which processes, data, applications, integrations, security, AI, legal/policy, risk, or industry factors matter?
11. **Feedback and recognition:** Who reviews the work and what evidence earns recognition?
12. **Safety and governance:** What confidentiality, fairness, supplier-risk, approval, recordkeeping, and AI safeguards apply?
13. **Transfer:** How will the learner apply the method to a different category or supplier scenario?
14. **Growth loop:** What approved artifact or lesson should improve the curriculum next?

## 16. Recommended Architecture for Autonomous Procurement Curriculum Design

Treat the curriculum as a connected system with reusable records:

1. **Learner Profile:** target role, experience, capability baseline, chosen procurement domain, industry context, and learning goals.
2. **Business Need Catalogue:** stakeholder, need, constraints, requirements, success criteria, and approval pathway.
3. **Procurement Scenario Catalogue:** category, fictional suppliers, offers, contract summaries, spend data, risks, and data provenance.
4. **Outcome Catalogue:** procurement problem, intended outcome, value hypotheses, acceptance criteria, and measurement method.
5. **Format Catalogue:** purpose, depth, duration, prerequisites, interaction pattern, expected output, and what the format is not.
6. **Mission Catalogue:** actions, artifact, reference evidence, rubric, control requirements, and safety notes.
7. **Domain-to-Lab Capability Map:** mapping between procurement domains, the 20 learning labs, and relevant competencies.
8. **Evidence Portfolio:** versioned submissions, evaluation rationale, peer feedback, test evidence, reflection, and mastery decisions.
9. **Improvement Backlog:** unclear instructions, inconsistent scoring, missing prerequisites, recurring process misconceptions, and candidate mission improvements.
10. **Governance Catalogue:** data classification, supplier confidentiality, conflicts, evaluation rules, approval boundaries, and AI safeguards.

A recommendation engine can suggest the next mission based on learner goals and demonstrated gaps. Show the rationale, permit learner/mentor override, and do not infer procurement competence from clicks, streaks, or speed alone.

### Suggested end-to-end loop

**Choose procurement problem → define winning promise → select learning format → complete missions → review commercial/process/control evidence → recognise mastery → transfer to another supplier/category context → improve the curriculum.**

## 17. Recommended Next Steps

1. Pilot one bounded challenge, such as **supplier evaluation** or **supplier onboarding risk**, rather than gamifying every procurement topic at once.
2. Design one Webinar, one Workshop, and one Sprint around the same fictional case to test awareness, skill practice, and artifact completion.
3. Add a Bootcamp or Hackathon only after the mission instructions, criteria, and review process are working consistently.
4. Make the evaluation criteria, data pack, risk assumptions, and expected artifacts explicit.
5. Validate the pilot with procurement practitioners and relevant stakeholders, then map the proven pattern to formal streams already defined in this repository.
6. Adapt scenarios to industry, sourcing context, supplier market, procurement policy, and operating-model differences.
7. Maintain versioned artifacts and document why each curriculum change was made.

## Core Message

**Autonomous Procurement is not simply a library of procurement content. It is a progression of missions through which learners clarify business needs, design sourcing strategies, evaluate suppliers, manage contracts, improve purchasing processes, connect spend data, strengthen controls, and experiment responsibly with automation and AI.**

The six-part Gameboard provides the learning progression. The eight formats provide different ways to learn and act. Procurement domains provide the business contexts. The 20 SuccessLabs labs provide transformation pathways. Industry scenarios make the work realistic. The evidence portfolio makes capability visible.

**The desired transformation:** learners move from processing purchase requests to making defensible procurement decisions, managing supplier and contract outcomes, validating end-to-end processes, and improving business value with evidence and ownership.
