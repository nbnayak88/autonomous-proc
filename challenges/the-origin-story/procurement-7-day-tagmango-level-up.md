### History and future editorial note

Procurement existed long before 1970. The verified historical anchor in this pack is SAP's 1974 purchasing-software development and 1975 materials management integration [1]. Other “picture 1970” scenes are illustrative learning scenarios, not universal historical claims. Present-day scenes are exercises, and all 2050 scenes are imagined possibilities rather than forecasts. The evolution of tools does not imply that earlier buyers lacked judgement, ethics or lifecycle thinking.

## Day 1 — Every Purchase Shapes a Future

🎯 **DAY 1 OF 7 — THE PROCUREMENT MINDSET SHIFT**

Welcome! Today, begin with a purchase you already understand.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes: read, reflect and share.**

### 💭 Which chair would you choose?

One costs ₹3,000. Another costs ₹5,000.

Before choosing, you might ask: Is it comfortable? Will it last? Can it be repaired? Who will use it?

You have already started thinking about procurement.

**Procurement means understanding a need, choosing a suitable supplier and making sure the purchase delivers value.**

### 🕰️ A brief journey: 1970 → 2050

**1970s — Connecting the purchase.** SAP began developing purchasing software in 1974. Its 1975 materials management system linked purchasing, inventory and invoice verification [1]. One important possibility was connecting buying with other business records.

**Today — Connect the decision to its consequences.** Alongside price, ask about reliability, suitability, working conditions and waste. Real supplier policies can include social and environmental expectations [2].

**Toward 2050 — Imagine buying with foresight.** AI could help compare options while people decide what matters and remain accountable. What should our buying decisions support? This is a possible future, not a prediction.

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“What is the cheapest option?” → “What choice best meets the need, and what happens because I choose it?”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Refreshments for a community event affect participants, a supplier's livelihood and food waste. The purchase is small; its consequences reach beyond your wallet.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**The best purchase might be no purchase.** Repairing, borrowing, renting or reusing may meet the need.

### ✍️ Your 5-minute action

Choose a recent or planned purchase. Reply in text:

- **My purchase:** ___
- **The need it solves:** ___
- **Beyond price, one thing that matters:** ___
- **One person or environmental impact to consider:** ___

**Example:** A workspace chair; comfortable seating while I work; repairability; whether repairing my existing chair would reduce waste.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Keep your “need” sentence. It is the origin of your later requirements and checks. If useful, label it **NEED-01**; the label is optional.

🎉 **Today's win:** You can explain why a purchase matters.

## Day 2 — Spot the Spend: Every Rupee Tells a Story

🔍 **DAY 2 OF 7 — BECOME A SPEND DETECTIVE**

Yesterday, you explored a need. Today, investigate where money goes.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. No spreadsheet required.**

### 💭 A familiar moment

A subscription renews. Then someone asks: **“When did you last use it?”**

Before changing your spending, understand it. A transaction is a clue, not a verdict.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

**1970s:** The integration example from Day 1 connected purchasing, stock and invoice records [1]. “What did we buy?”

**Today:** Explore your bills or subscription history. “Are we using what we pay for?”

**Toward 2050:** Imagine an assistant flagging duplicate subscriptions before renewal. “What should we investigate before the next payment?” This is an imagined possibility; you still judge the recommendation.

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“The bill is paid.” → “What useful result did this spending create?”**

### 🌍 People and impact

A learning community might redirect money from a duplicate tool to accessible learning materials. Before cutting spending, check who depends on it and who could be affected.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**Unused does not always mean unnecessary.** A backup or safety service may be rarely used and still be valuable.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Choose a personal, community or fictional business budget. List three recurring expenses using the same period, such as monthly. Estimates are fine.

Label each:

- **Keep:** Purpose and value are clear.
- **Review:** More information is needed.
- **Explore:** Another option may meet the need better.

Reply:

- **My area:** ___
- **Expense 1:** ₹___/month — Keep / Review / Explore
- **Expense 2:** ₹___/month — Keep / Review / Explore
- **Expense 3:** ₹___/month — Keep / Review / Explore
- **One pattern I noticed:** ___

Divide annual charges by 12 for a monthly estimate. Do not treat a one-time purchase as a monthly recurring cost.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Which expense supports your Day 1 need? If your original purchase is not recurring, keep it and use today's review as context. You may switch your main example today; explain why, then carry it forward.

🎉 **Today's win:** Three payments have become questions worth exploring.

## Day 3 — Three Questions Before You Buy

🔍 **DAY 3 OF 7 — NEED, VALUE AND RISK**

You have noticed spending patterns. Now test one decision.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Short answers are enough.**

### 💭 Which printer is the better choice?

A ₹4,000 printer looks attractive. But what if ink is costly and repairs are difficult to arrange?

You need more information.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

These are illustrative scenes, not a universal history.

**Picture 1970:** A buyer checks a request before ordering. “What do we need, and can we afford it?”

**Today:** Connect the need with running costs and support. “Will this meet our need reliably?”

**Imagine 2050:** AI flags problems in an offer. “Is the evidence trustworthy, and are the trade-offs acceptable?” People still decide.

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“This looks like a good deal.” → “I can explain why this is a good decision.”**

**1. NEED — What problem am I solving?**  
What happens if you do not buy it? Could repair, borrowing or an existing resource help?

**2. VALUE — What useful result will I get?**  
Consider suitability, quality, time saved and ongoing costs alongside price.

**3. RISK — What could go wrong?**  
Consider delay, breakdown, unavailable supplies or poor support. What is your backup?

### 🌍 People and impact

For event refreshments, the need includes feeding participants. Value includes suitable portions and dietary needs. Risks include unsafe food or late delivery. Better questions protect the people depending on your decision.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**“I need more information” is a useful result.** You do not have to force a decision.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Use your main example, informed by Day 2. Reply:

- **My expense or purchase:** ___
- **Need — The problem it solves:** ___
- **Value — What makes it worthwhile:** ___
- **Risk — What could go wrong, and my backup:** ___
- **My next move:** Keep / Change / Pause / Gather information
- **Because:** ___

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Compare your answer with your Day 1 need. Has it become clearer? Keep the original and note the revision. Later, this habit helps us trace why a requirement changed.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a reasoned next move.

Tomorrow: **Your First Supplier Request**—make your need clear to someone else.

## Day 4 — A Better Answer Starts with a Clearer Ask

📝 **DAY 4 OF 7 — YOUR FIRST MINI RFQ**

Turn your Need, Value and Risk thinking into a supplier request.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Drafting is enough; no supplier contact needed.**

### 💭 Compare these requests

**A:** “Send your best price for chairs.”

**B:** “Please quote for 10 adjustable chairs with back support, delivered and assembled within three weeks. Include taxes, delivery charges and warranty.”

Clarity helps both sides.

A structured request for price and terms is called an **RFQ: Request for Quotation**. It works best when the requirement is clear enough for comparable offers.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

These are illustrative scenes.

**Picture 1970:** A typed enquiry needs a follow-up call because quantity is missing.

**Today:** The same clear digital request goes to several suppliers.

**Imagine 2050:** An assistant flags missing quantities or conflicting requirements before sending. You still check that the request reflects the need.

The enduring question: **“Can someone respond accurately to what I have asked?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“They should know what I mean.” → “I will explain what a useful response needs to contain.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Clear requirements and reasonable deadlines respect suppliers' time. Ask for relevant information, and give smaller suppliers a fair opportunity to respond.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**Describe the outcome before prescribing the product.** “Participants must clearly see the learning materials” may reveal options beyond buying a new projector.

If the solution is unclear, ask for suggestions before requesting comparable prices.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Complete this mini RFQ:

- **What I need and why:** ___
- **Quantity and general delivery location:** ___
- **Required by:** ___
- **Must-haves:** ___
- **Please include:** Price, applicable taxes, delivery/setup charges, delivery date, warranty/support and quote validity.
- **One way I will check the result:** ___

**Example:** Ten adjustable chairs for a learning room in Gurugram, delivered within three weeks of order confirmation; assembly included and at least a one-year warranty. Check that all ten are assembled and height adjustment works before use.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Link one must-have to your original need. Your “way I will check” is the beginning of an **acceptance check**—something observable that shows a requirement was met.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a request someone can act on and a first idea of how to check delivery.

## Day 5 — Compare Suppliers: Make Your Priorities Visible

⚖️ **DAY 5 OF 7 — YOUR FIRST SUPPLIER SCORECARD**

Two suppliers reply. One is cheaper; the other offers stronger support. How will you choose?

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Your reasoning matters more than arithmetic.**

### 🧠 Today's lesson

A **weighted scorecard** gives more influence to the things that matter most.

First check your non-negotiable requirements from Day 4. Clarify any essential gap before scoring.

Then score each relevant criterion from **1 = poor fit to 5 = excellent fit**. For price, a higher score means better affordability for equivalent requirements. Set weights before scoring; they must total 100%.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** Quotations sit side by side on a desk. “Which meets the requirement?”

**Today:** A shared scorecard makes priorities discussable. “Can someone understand our reasoning?”

**Imagine 2050:** AI organises offers and flags missing evidence. “Are we measuring what matters, and can we trust the evidence?”

### 🌍 People and impact

Use consistent, relevant criteria. A familiar brand should not automatically receive a higher score; assess evidence of ability to deliver.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**A precise number can hide a weak assumption.** If support is unverified, write “unknown” and ask a question. Do not quietly turn missing information into a confident score.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Compare two real or fictional options. Alternatively, discuss this pre-scored practice example:

| Criterion | Weight | Supplier A | Supplier B |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Price | 25% | 5 | 3 |
| Quality and suitability | 30% | 3 | 4 |
| Delivery | 20% | 4 | 4 |
| Reliability and support | 15% | 3 | 4 |
| Risk and backup arrangements | 10% | 2 | 4 |
| **Weighted total out of 5** | **100%** | **3.60** | **3.75** |

These are fictional scores, not actual supplier evidence. Calculate each contribution as score × weight; for example, 5 × 25% = 1.25. Add the contributions.

Supplier B scores slightly higher under these assumptions. The score supports a decision; it does not automatically settle it.

Reply:

- **My purchase:** ___
- **My most important criterion and why:** ___
- **My preferred supplier:** ___
- **Evidence supporting my choice, or assumptions in the practice example:** ___
- **One thing I still need to check:** ___

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Which Day 4 requirement supports your most important criterion? If you cannot explain the link, revisit the criterion.

🎉 **Today's win:** Your priorities and reasoning are visible.

## Day 6 — The Hidden Cost Trap: Look Beyond the Price Tag

🔍 **DAY 6 OF 7 — WHAT WILL IT COST TO KEEP WORKING?**

Yesterday, you chose a preferred option. Today, test that choice against costs beyond the quote.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Simple addition is enough.**

### 💭 Which printer would you choose?

Fictional example, assuming the same printing volume:

| Cost over one year | Printer A | Printer B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Purchase price | ₹4,000 | ₹7,000 |
| Ink | ₹6,000 | ₹2,000 |
| Delivery and setup | ₹500 | ₹500 |
| **Estimated one-year cost** | **₹10,500** | **₹9,500** |

Under these assumptions, Printer B costs ₹1,000 less over one year. Different usage could change the result.

**Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)** considers buying, using, maintaining and eventually retiring something. This one-year example is a starting point, not a complete lifetime calculation.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes, without implying that earlier buyers ignored running costs:

**Picture 1970:** An equipment buyer checks fuel, spares and repairs.

**Today:** Connect delivery, setup, training, maintenance and disposal where relevant.

**Imagine 2050:** An assistant helps compare repair, reuse and recovery scenarios. People still check the assumptions.

The enduring question: **“What happens after we pay the invoice?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“Can I afford to buy it?” → “Can I afford to use and maintain it?”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Breakdowns interrupt work. Difficult-to-repair products may become waste sooner. Ask who maintains the purchase, who experiences downtime and what happens at the end of its useful life.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**Free can still require effort.** A free tool may take hours to set up or operate. Record time separately when you cannot reasonably value it in money.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Return to your Day 5 choice. Reply:

- **My purchase and comparison period:** ___
- **Upfront price:** ₹___
- **Other relevant costs in this period:** ₹___ for ___
- **Estimated total:** ₹___
- **One uncertain cost or risk to investigate:** ___
- **My decision stays the same / changes because:** ___

Label estimates. Write “unknown” where needed. Keep uncertain breakdowns separate from confirmed costs unless you explain your estimation. For existing purchases or subscriptions, do not add an upfront cost again if it does not apply to your comparison.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Does your cost estimate still support your original need and budget? Record why a decision changed. Do not rewrite the earlier evidence as though you always knew.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have tested whether your preferred option still makes sense.

## Day 7 — Your Procurement Origin Story

🗺️ **DAY 7 OF 7 — MAKE, EXPLAIN AND IMPROVE A DECISION**

You have explored a need, reviewed spend, asked questions, drafted a request, compared options and investigated costs.

Today, connect the pieces.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Your own words are enough.**

### 💭 Return to your starting point

Would you buy the same thing, choose another supplier, repair something—or gather more evidence?

Your choice may be unchanged. Your reasons may be stronger. Both can show learning.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** Turn a business need into a dependable order.

**Today:** Connect users, budgets, suppliers, support and consequences.

**Imagine 2050:** AI handles more routine comparisons while people set boundaries and remain accountable. “What should we optimise, and whose interests should we protect?”

This future is a possibility you can help shape.

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“I completed a buying task.” → “I can make, explain and improve a buying decision.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Success can include staying within budget, receiving safe products on time, reducing waste and paying a supplier as agreed. Define the benefit for people, not just the saving.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**Changing your mind can be your strongest result.** New evidence is a reason to learn, not something to hide.

### 🧵 Your first experience of traceability

Your work now connects:

**Original need → requirements → decision → checks → readiness → outcome.**

A **traceability matrix** records these links. In later projects, it helps us ask:

- During requirement gathering: “Which need does this requirement serve?”
- During testing: “Which requirement does this check verify, and what evidence do we have?”
- Before go-live: “Are the critical requirements checked, and are unresolved issues understood?”
- After use: “Did the intended benefit actually happen?”

The matrix supports readiness decisions alongside other checks. A purchase or system can meet its specification without yet proving the intended human benefit.

## Coach-only appendix — From Origin Story to traceability

### Keep the introduction light

Do not ask beginners to maintain a formal matrix during this challenge. Their daily text submissions create the starting evidence. Later bootcamps can introduce IDs, ownership, test results, defects, change history and approvals.

The Origin Story explains purpose and context. It does not replace stakeholder validation, measurable requirements or agreed acceptance criteria.

### Example: learning-room chairs

Illustrative planned records only; no procurement, test, readiness approval or outcome below has actually occurred.

**OS-01 — Origin Story:** Workshop participants need a learning room with usable, supportive seating.  
**NEED-01 — Validated need to establish:** Ten participants need suitable chairs available before the workshop. Confirm suitability with intended users.

| Origin / need | Requirement | Decision / evidence to collect | Test or acceptance check | Go-live / use readiness | Outcome evidence after use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-01: Ten assembled chairs available before the agreed workshop date | DEC-01: Supplier choice; written quantity, delivery and assembly commitment | TEST-01: Count assembled chairs; record delivery date and inspection results | READY-01: All ten available before opening, or an agreed alternative with owner approval | OUT-01: Record whether participants had usable seating |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-02: Height adjustment and back support meet the specifications agreed with intended users | DEC-01: Product specifications and sample evaluation | TEST-02: Verify adjustment on each chair and evaluate agreed suitability criteria | READY-02: Resolve or explicitly manage any failed checks before use | OUT-02: Gather participant feedback; do not treat specification compliance as proof of comfort |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-03: At least a one-year warranty with a documented support route | DEC-01: Written warranty and supplier contact | TEST-03: Inspect warranty documentation and confirm the support contact | READY-03: Owner knows how to report faults | OUT-03: Record faults and supplier response if any occur |

**Initial status for every record:** Proposed / not yet verified. Assign real owners, dates and evidence links during implementation. Do not present planned evidence as completed evidence.

### What the matrix should help reveal

- A requirement with no agreed need or stakeholder rationale.
- A requirement without a test, or a test without a relevant requirement.
- A failed test or unresolved defect affecting readiness.
- A decision change that affects requirements, tests, costs or dates.
- A technically accepted solution without evidence of the intended outcome.

Traceability should work in both directions. From a need, find its requirements, checks and results. From a test, feature or purchase, find the need it supports. Not every relationship is one-to-one.

An ID is a stable reference, not proof. Retain evidence, status, owner and revision history. When the story changes, assess the affected links rather than silently overwriting the earlier reasoning.

### Transfer to the other six domains

| Domain | Example starting story | Later traceability connection |
|---|---|---|
| HR | New joiners struggle to become ready for work | Employee need → onboarding requirements → configured journey → user acceptance tests → readiness → experience outcomes |
| CX | Customers repeat their problem across service channels | Customer need → service requirements → journey changes → scenario tests → channel readiness → resolution outcomes |
| Finance | Teams lack timely, reliable cost information | Decision need → reporting requirements → data and controls → reconciliation tests → close/report readiness → decision usefulness |
| SCM | Late deliveries disrupt operations | Operational need → planning/logistics requirements → solution → fulfilment tests → operational readiness → delivery outcomes |
| Business Transformation Management | A change initiative lacks a clear intended benefit | Purpose → capabilities and requirements → interventions → validation → readiness → benefit review |
| Leadership & Identity Transformation | A learner wants to lead more consistently with their values | Aspiration → chosen behaviours → experiments → observation → readiness for the next responsibility → reflection and feedback |

For leadership, use traceability as a voluntary reflection aid, not as a claim that identity or human worth can be reduced to a compliance score.

- Acknowledge the learner's reasoning, then ask one question that deepens it.
